A public clock is a claim. Someone mounted it on a wall, at expense, so that
strangers passing underneath would agree about when they were. It is
infrastructure for a shared present tense.

Most of them are wrong. Not dramatically — a few minutes fast, a few slow,
stopped at some hour nobody witnessed. Walk a mile through any English city
centre reading the clocks and you collect a set of contradictory statements
about the same instant, all of them public, none of them corrected.

I spend my working life with measurements that are only meaningful when the
uncertainty on them is stated. A clock face states nothing. It asserts. And a
city full of asserting, disagreeing faces is a reasonable picture of how time
is actually experienced at street level — not as a coordinate, but as an
argument nobody is having.

These were made on foot, at the hours when a clock face is the brightest thing
on a dark wall.
